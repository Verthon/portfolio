<Heading tag='h2' id='organization-context' linkLabel='Link to heading: Organization context'>Organization context</Heading>

Big platform for managing and creating establishments

- 10+ million users
- 30+ frontend apps
- 300+ developers from multiple companies
- 110+ components in the single design-system package
- Single Page Application based frontend architecture

My team didn't own the design system. We worked as a frontend platform team, enabling product (stream-aligned) teams to focus on their product, rather than the surrounding infrastructure.  
We were the first integrators of the DS. A ~1-year early-adopter phase helped us establish a list of the DS gaps.

<Heading tag="h2" id="design-system-goals" linkLabel="Link to heading: Design system goals">Design system goals</Heading>

What the DS must deliver for the business. These goals define what 'good' looks like and what we hold the DS accountable for.

- Consistent UI/UX across the entire platform
- Faster time-to-market for product (stream-aligned) teams
- Lower total cost of ownership through reusable building blocks
- WCAG compliance and reduced legal/brand risk
- Scales to new brands and markets (multi-brand ready)
- Faster onboarding and practical accessibility training

<Heading tag="h2" id="design-system-pitfalls" linkLabel="Link to heading: Three decisions that became every app's problem">Three decisions that became every app's problem</Heading>

Each of these was something the DS treated as internal, and consumers depended on anyway. That's [Hyrum's Law](https://www.hyrumslaw.com/) from the consumer's side. The owner's side is in [API surface challenges on a shared platform](/blog/api-surface-challenges/).

<Heading tag="h3" id="accessibility-fix-shipped-as-a-minor" linkLabel="Link to heading: An accessibility fix shipped as a minor">An accessibility fix shipped as a minor</Heading>

The DS came with a reference app showing how its components fit together on a real page.

A minor release moved Button from `disabled` to `aria-disabled="true"`, with clicks blocked inside the component. The change was right for accessibility. Teams were under pressure to ship a11y fixes, and it went out as non-breaking, as if the attribute were an internal detail.

After the bump, the unit tests in my service failed. jest-dom's `toBeDisabled` ignores `aria-disabled` by design ([jest-dom #144](https://github.com/testing-library/jest-dom/issues/144)). As far as I could tell, the app itself was fine.

```tsx
// before
expect(button).toBeDisabled()
// after
expect(button).toHaveAttribute('aria-disabled', 'true')
```

I rewrote about 15 assertions by hand. The minor also broke its own promise that upgrading is safe. There was no migration note, so every team with `toBeDisabled` assertions hit the same failures and fixed them alone.

What would have caught it:

- A major version, with the migration above in the changelog.
- The reference app with tests written the way consumers write theirs. The DS's CI would have failed before the release.

<Heading tag="h3" id="styled-components-as-a-shared-singleton" linkLabel="Link to heading: styled-components as a shared singleton">styled-components as a shared singleton</Heading>

The DS styled its components with styled-components. That choice predates micro-frontends, and it was reasonable then. The DS promise was that consumers never write CSS, so the styling engine counted as internal.

Then the platform moved to micro-frontends. Header, sidebar and footer are separate projects, loaded into every app with module federation. styled-components had to be shared as a [singleton](https://module-federation.io/configure/shared#singleton) between each app and those micro-frontends.

No app could upgrade it on its own, and the platform team avoided touching it. Teams also wrote local style overrides, so consumers wrote CSS after all.

We asked the DS team to move to Panda CSS. Its syntax is close to styled-components, and it has a [migration guide](https://panda-css.com/docs/migration/styled-components). We offered to help. The answer was no: no time, too big, not enough people. The DS wasn't open to outside contributions, and org politics played a part.

Looking back, I think "too big" was the rational answer. The DS had no browser or visual tests, so nobody could prove 110+ components looked the same after a migration. [Linear's move off styled-components](https://www.skovhus.dev/blog/moving-linear-from-styled-components-to-stylex) needed ~100k lines of tooling and was 58% done after about six months.

As of writing, the DS still hasn't migrated. We configure the singleton in one place. Upgrading it would be a disaster, so we don't try. The platform team keeps a dependency it doesn't own and waits for the DS to remove it.

What would have prevented it:

- Re-evaluating the DS's runtime dependencies when the architecture changed.
- Browser and visual tests on the DS, so a styling migration can be verified.

<Heading tag="h3" id="react-pdf-webpack-entry-in-every-consumer" linkLabel="Link to heading: react-pdf's Webpack entry in every consumer">react-pdf's Webpack entry in every consumer</Heading>

Some DS components render PDFs with react-pdf. The DS imported its Webpack 4 entry, the setup the [react-pdf v5 README](https://github.com/wojtekmaj/react-pdf/blob/v5.x/README.md#configure-pdfjs-worker) recommended for Webpack users:

```ts
import { Document, Page } from 'react-pdf/dist/esm/entry.webpack'
```

The DS treated that entry as its own build detail. Our apps ran Vitest and Rsbuild, and the first test that touched a PDF component failed:

```
failed to resolve import worker-loader!./pdf.worker.entry.js
```

We needed two workarounds: an alias back to the plain `react-pdf` entry for Vitest, and react-pdf in [Rsbuild `externals`](https://rsbuild.rs/config/output/externals) for the build. Both live in the shared scripts package from our [Rsbuild migration](/blog/webpack-to-rsbuild-migration/), so every app got the fix as soon as we found the problem.

The DS team had no capacity to upgrade react-pdf, so the workarounds stayed. Then the CI security scanner flagged an advisory in the old version. Every app had to add its own `overrides` entry in `package.json` to force a newer react-pdf major, one the DS had never been tested against, on top of the bundler and Vitest workarounds. One fix in the DS would have removed all of it.

With no browser tests, nobody trusted a react-pdf upgrade in the DS. It came months later, pushed by the advisory and by us wanting the workarounds gone.

What would have caught it:

- A reference app on the same stack as consumers, with tests. The DS's reference app ran on the DS team's own setup.
- No bundler-specific entry imports in the DS.

<Heading tag="h2" id="what-to-establish-first" linkLabel="Link to heading: What to establish first">What to establish first</Heading>

The DS was still worth having. Storybook was the single source of docs, the reference app existed, and office hours worked. At 30+ apps, I'd take it with these flaws over no DS.

If you own a DS:

- Treat behaviour that consumer tests can see as API. Changing it is a major, with a migration note.
- Keep a reference app on your consumers' stack, with tests.
- Don't import bundler-specific entries.
- Re-evaluate runtime dependencies when the architecture changes.

If you integrate one:

- Keep every workaround in one package you own.
- Configure shared singletons in one place.
- When you ask the DS team to migrate, bring a way to verify the result, not only a path. Our migration guide and offer of help had no tests behind them, and "too big" was the rational no.

<Heading tag="h2" id="other-gaps" linkLabel="Link to heading: Other gaps">Other gaps</Heading>

**Organization**

- A core DS team of two, with no contribution model. That's the "no capacity" behind styled-components and react-pdf. Brad Frost's [ecosystem layers](https://bradfrost.com/blog/post/the-design-system-ecosystem/) are one way to plan contributions upfront.
- Figma files used frames instead of library components, so design and code drifted.
- No KPIs for the DS, so nobody could show whether it met the goals above.

**Architecture**

- The DS tried to cover every case. Components went through review, but each one grew a prop per consumer variant, some existed for only a few clients, and the package passed 110. Tokens and base components weren't exposed, so composing and styling your own variant to the design, shadcn-style, wasn't an option.
- No automated a11y checks in the DS's tests, although its most disruptive release was an a11y fix.
- No bundle-size budgets.
- No dead-code detection, such as [knip](https://knip.dev/).
- TypeScript that could have been stricter.
